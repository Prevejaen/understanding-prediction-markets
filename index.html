Understanding Prediction Markets and How They Work

By Preveja · October 2026 · 4 min read

Every day people argue about what will happen next: who wins an election, whether a central bank cuts rates, whether a new law passes. Most of that debate disappears into comment threads and group chats. A prediction market gives it structure, a place where a view about the future becomes a number that anyone can read, compare and watch change over time.

This article walks through the basic ideas without jargon: what a market is, how a price relates to a probability, what happens when the event is over, and what to keep in mind before taking part.

A market is a question with an end date

Every market starts with a question that can be answered clearly with Yes or No, plus a date by which it must be answered. "Will the Copom cut the Selic rate at its next meeting?" is a good example: the outcome will be public, the date is known, and nobody can argue afterwards about what the result was. Vague questions make poor markets, which is why wording and resolution rules matter as much as the topic itself. On Preveja, each market page shows the question, the closing date and the current Yes and No prices, so you can see exactly how the market is framed before you do anything else.

Why a price can be read as a probability

In a typical market, a Yes share pays out if the event happens and nothing if it does not. If participants collectively believe an outcome has a 55 percent chance, the Yes side tends to trade around that level. The multiplier shown beside it is the same idea turned upside down: a figure of 1.82x corresponds to a probability near 55 percent, because one divided by 1.82 is roughly 0.55. Once you see that link, every card on the page becomes a quick summary of what the crowd currently thinks.

Prices move because information moves

A market is not a static poll. When a new survey is published, a court schedules a hearing or a company announces results, people revise their opinions and the price follows. That is what makes markets useful for forecasting: the number reflects not only what people feel but also how willing they are to act on that feeling.

A lopsided price with very little activity deserves caution, because a handful of participants can move it. A price that holds steady while plenty of activity passes through it carries more weight. Volume, shown on each card, is therefore worth reading alongside the percentage.

What happens when the event ends

When the real-world outcome is known, the market resolves. Holders of the winning side receive the payout and the losing side receives nothing. Because the rules are written in advance, the result does not depend on anyone's opinion. That is also why you should read the resolution details before taking a position: the source used to decide the outcome, and any edge cases, are stated up front. Markets that close in minutes resolve almost instantly, while political questions can stay open for months.

A few common misunderstandings

A market is not simply a poll, because participants back their opinion with money, which tends to discourage casual guesses. A high percentage is not a guarantee either; an outcome priced at 70 percent still fails three times in ten. And a new market sitting at 50 percent with no volume is not broken. It usually means nobody has expressed a view yet, not that the event is a coin flip.

Practical habits for newcomers

Start by reading rather than trading. Watch a few markets move around news events and compare the price with your own estimate. Keep stakes small while you learn how pricing behaves, and treat a market as a forecast rather than a promise. Check that the service is permitted where you live, and never use money you cannot afford to lose. Many people use markets purely for information, following the numbers the way others follow a weather forecast.

The short version

A prediction market turns a clear question into a price, the price into a probability, and the final result into a payout. Understanding those three steps is enough to read almost any market with confidence. From there, the best way to learn is to pick a topic you already follow and watch how the number responds to the news.
